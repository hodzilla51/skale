## 2. Task shape observed in 2009

The problem concerns genetic-treatment clinical research at a public prefectural university.

X, a professor leading a clinical gene-therapy research group, is affected by two different university actions:

1. the medical-school dean orders the clinical research to stop after an unforeseen serious event leaves participant C critically ill;
2. the university later suspends X for one month because X disclosed genetic information concerning C and C's family in violation of the university's genetic-information rule.

The examinee must address each action separately from X's lawyer's perspective, anticipate the university's justification, and give an independently reasoned conclusion.

The official intent emphasizes that the question does not seek a stock list of precedent and doctrine. It expects an accurate doctrinal foundation, a carefully constructed analytical framework, and individualized analysis of the concrete facts.

## 3. Examiner-required analytical operations

### O1 — Keep the two sanctions and their constitutional problems separate

The research-stop order and the suspension arise from different conduct, different rules, and different constitutional interests.

Do not collapse them into one generic "academic freedom" problem.

The structure of the problem itself requires routing each measure to its own constitutional analysis.

### O2 — Identify the protected interest and the actual right-holder before analyzing the restriction

For the research-stop order, the central protected interest is X's freedom of academic research under Article 23.

For the information-disclosure suspension, the direct constitutional interests implicated by the underlying rule are primarily C's interest in access to C's own genetic information and the privacy / informational interests of C's family members.

Because X is the person sanctioned while some relevant constitutional interests belong to C or C's family, the answer must not assume that right-holder and litigant are the same person.

### O3 — Separate rule-level constitutional attack from application / disposition-level attack

The examiner materials expressly identify both:

- constitutional defects in the university rules themselves; and
- constitutional defects in the concrete research-stop or suspension decision.

The answer should route arguments to the correct level rather than mixing abstract defects in the rule with facts unique to X's case.

### O4 — Characterize the specific form of freedom before importing a review framework

The research in question is experimental clinical research involving intervention in a human subject.

The examiner materials emphasize that this has a different factual and normative character from thought-centered academic inquiry and that the restriction is not a prohibition of communicative content.

Therefore, an answer should not mechanically import speech-content review simply because academic freedom is a spiritual freedom.

### O5 — Analyze the relation between individual research freedom and university autonomy

Article 23 protects the research freedom of the individual researcher, while university autonomy is conventionally understood as an institutional guarantee supporting academic freedom.

In this problem, however, the two point in opposite directions: university autonomy may be invoked to justify institutional control over the researcher's project.

The answer must therefore explain whether and how an institutional guarantee normally designed to protect academic freedom can justify restricting an individual researcher's freedom.

### O6 — Analyze why the university rule may be stricter than the national guideline

The national gene-therapy guideline used a flexible, non-penal framework, while the university adopted a rule permitting research cessation after serious events.

The official intent warns that this is not simply a statute-versus-ordinance problem and not a delegation problem.

The answer should instead consider the institutional reasons for a university-specific stricter rule, including autonomy, responsibility for approved research, ethics, and the factual background that prompted the change.

### O7 — Build the review framework from the concrete conflict rather than selecting a canned tier

The grading commentary criticizes a pattern in which the examinee gives X strict scrutiny, the university rational-basis review, and the examinee an intermediate standard, without actually analyzing the case.

Relevant concrete considerations include:

- importance of research freedom;
- the character of advanced clinical research;
- risk to the participant's life and body;
- uncertainty and unforeseeable harm;
- participant consent and the limits of relying on that consent;
- university responsibility and ethics;
- fit between the institutional rule and the actual risk.

### O8 — Locate the informational right in the correct constitutional relationship

The problem uses a "right to know" type interest, but not in the ordinary sender / recipient relationship associated with Article 21.

For access to one's own genetic information, the official intent directs attention toward Article 13 and a self-information-control / informational-privacy conception, including a possible information-disclosure claim.

The answer must therefore determine what the interest actually is before choosing the constitutional source and framework.

### O9 — Split C's own information from the family's information

Two distinct information problems exist:

1. restricting C's access to C's own genetic information;
2. restricting C's access to genetic information belonging to C's family members.

The first raises the justification for withholding a person's own information.

The second creates a conflict between C's interest in receiving information and the family members' privacy / informational interests.

These are not the same problem merely because genetic information is shared within a family.

### O10 — Evaluate paternalism explicitly

The rule withheld some of C's own genetic information partly to avoid negative effects on C from learning it.

The examiner materials characterize this as a paternalistic reason.

The answer should ask whether protection of an autonomous adult from self-harm or psychological harm is a constitutionally sufficient justification for withholding the adult's own information, rather than treating paternalism as automatically valid or invalid.

### O11 — Analyze conflict with third-party privacy concretely

X disclosed genetic information concerning C's family without their consent.

The answer should identify the privacy / informational interest of those family members and assess the competing reasons for C to receive the information.

The mere fact that the people are family members does not by itself erase or substantially reduce their privacy interests.

### O12 — Address third-party-right invocation / constitutional standing

Because X is sanctioned but may need to rely on constitutional interests belonging to C or C's family, the answer must address whether X may assert a constitutional violation based on the rights of specific third parties.

The official intent requires use and examination of the precedent framework on third-party constitutional claims rather than uncritically treating one formula as exhaustive.

### O13 — Use informed consent and explanation duties in both directions

C's consent to risky treatment supports respect for C's autonomous choice, but the examiner materials also permit questioning how much weight should be placed on consent in highly technical experimental medicine.

For the disclosure issue, the materials specifically identify X's failure to explain the university rule that conflicted with C's demand for information as legally relevant.

The blood-transfusion-refusal precedent is signaled as an anchor for recognizing an explanation-duty / autonomy problem.

### O14 — Do not mechanically invoke the partial-society doctrine

A university may appear to be an internal institutional setting, but the examiner materials warn against automatically using the partial-society doctrine to exclude judicial review.

If that doctrine is raised, the answer must examine whether the precedent framework, including the Toyama University setting, should apply to this kind of sanction and whether the doctrine itself is appropriate here.

### O15 — Integrate the opposing position instead of writing the same answer three times

The university's strongest counterarguments should be realistic and responsive to X's position.

The examiner materials expressly state that the university's argument need not be developed as a fully separate duplicated essay. It can be integrated into the examinee's own analysis.

A weak opponent invented only to be easily defeated does not satisfy the task.

### O16 — Preserve factual integrity and obey problem-imposed routing constraints

The grading commentary identifies multiple failures caused by misreading or inventing facts.

Examples include:

- treating the prefectural university as a private actor and launching into private-party-effect analysis;
- confusing the basis of the research-stop order with the information-disclosure violation;
- treating C as having died when the problem states C recovered;
- assuming family consent to disclosure where none was given;
- making Article 31 procedure a central issue despite the problem's statement that prescribed procedures were carefully followed.

The operation is broader than "read carefully": facts and express exclusions must deactivate otherwise available doctrinal branches.

## 5. Strong grading-failure signals

### F1 — Stock doctrine / "automatic-vending-machine" answer

The official intent expressly rejects simply listing precedent and academic theories. Accurate doctrine is required, but it must be transformed into a framework that solves the concrete case.

### F2 — Review-standard template before factual analysis

The examiners criticize answers that jump immediately to a generic review standard and mechanically apply a few facts.

### F3 — Three-tier role-play without reasoning

Automatically assigning strict review to X, rational review to the university, and an intermediate test to the examinee is specifically criticized when unsupported by concrete analysis.

### F4 — Using a doctrine without understanding its native domain

Use of the "clear and present danger" formula without understanding its meaning and fit is identified as an example of inappropriate doctrinal transplantation.

### F5 — Misidentifying the right

In the second question, many answers failed to identify C's information interest and the family's privacy interest, instead invoking occupation, business, expression, or research-publication freedoms.

### F6 — Treating the right-to-know label as sufficient

Even when a right to know was noticed, answers were weak when they stayed in a generic Article 21 discussion and failed to address self-information, autonomy, paternalism, and informational privacy.

### F7 — Ignoring the university-autonomy conflict

A generic statement that university autonomy protects academic freedom does not solve the problem when the university invokes autonomy against the individual researcher's freedom.

### F8 — Interpreting the internal rule only and missing the constitutional question

Some answers focused on whether the event satisfied the phrase "serious event" without performing constitutional analysis.

### F9 — Failing to distinguish C's information from family information

Treating family status as automatic consent or as automatic reduction of privacy protection is specifically inadequate.

### F10 — Omitting third-party-right standing

Where X relies on C's or family members' constitutional interests, the answer must address whether X may do so.

### F11 — Mechanical partial-society doctrine

Using the Toyama University / partial-society framework as an automatic bar to judicial review without examining fit is insufficient.

### F12 — Repetitive adversarial structure

Repeating essentially the same material as X's position, Y's position, and the examinee's own view wastes answer space and does not demonstrate genuine engagement with the strongest opposing case.

### F13 — Fact invention or branch-routing error

Misreading the university's public status, the basis of each sanction, C's medical outcome, consent facts, or the problem's express procedural assumptions leads the answer into irrelevant doctrine.

## 6. Authorities / doctrinal anchors explicitly signaled by examiner materials

- Constitution Article 23 — freedom of academic research / academic freedom
- university autonomy and its institutional-guarantee relationship to academic freedom
- Constitution Article 13 — privacy, informational privacy, self-information control, personal autonomy
- the relation between Article 21-style "right to know" and a distinct claim concerning one's own information
- third-party constitutional-right invocation / standing precedent framework
- blood-transfusion-refusal precedent as a signal concerning explanation duties and personal autonomy
- Toyama University precedent / partial-society doctrine, with an express need to examine whether the framework fits
- distinction between rule-level invalidity and application / disposition-level unconstitutionality
- paternalistic restrictions as a justification problem

This list records signals visible in the examiner materials. It is not a complete doctrinal backfill.

## 7. Candidate year-level procedure

The following is a compact description of the **2009-required process**, not yet a constitutional parent model:

```text
Separate the challenged measures
→ identify the protected interest and actual right-holder for each
→ separate rule-level and application/disposition-level attacks
→ characterize the concrete form of the freedom or interest
→ identify the strongest countervailing constitutional / institutional interest
→ construct a case-specific review framework
→ translate supplied facts into legal reasons
→ resolve conflicts among autonomy, safety, institutional responsibility, and privacy
→ address third-party-right / judicial-review routing where relevant
→ integrate the strongest opposing case
→ give an independently reasoned conclusion
```

## 8. Particularly important SKALE observations from 2009

### 8.1 Legal labels are insufficient without semantic routing

The official materials repeatedly show that merely recognizing labels such as `学問の自由`, `知る権利`, `大学の自治`, or `審査基準` is not enough.

The examinee must first determine what kind of research, what kind of information interest, whose right, and what competing interest actually exists in this case.

### 8.2 A doctrine can reverse its ordinary directional role

University autonomy is normally taught as a structure supporting academic freedom. Here it may be used as a reason to restrict an individual researcher's freedom.

This suggests that a human-executable model should store the legal reason / relationship, not a brittle rule such as `大学の自治 -> 自由を強くする`.

### 8.3 One surface label may hide multiple distinct legal relationships

"Right to know" can refer to a classic Article 21 sender-recipient setting or to access to one's own sensitive information under an Article 13 autonomy / privacy theory.

Likewise, "genetic information" must be split according to whose information it is.

### 8.4 Explicit problem constraints should deactivate branches

The problem deliberately states facts such as careful compliance with prescribed procedure. A good model needs not only issue triggers but also **issue suppressors** that prevent reflexive issue accumulation.

### 8.5 Fact evaluation remains the central bridge

The grading commentary again treats factual analysis as more than copying facts into an application paragraph. The examinee must explain the legal meaning of each fact and integrate multiple facts into one coherent conclusion.
